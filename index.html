WHERE IT BEGAN

One language.
More than one meaning.

Growing up in Nigeria, I never really thought about how the words I used every day might sound to someone from another country. Expressions that felt completely natural to me could mean something very different to another listener. After moving to Canada, I began noticing those differences through moments that were sometimes awkward and sometimes funny. Two experiences in particular—a stranger questioning why I said “sorry” and a roommate misunderstanding a joke with my brother—made me think about how I use English. They helped me see that adapting my language does not always mean changing who I am; sometimes it means changing my words, and other times it means explaining them.

01 / THE ENCOUNTER

A word I never had to explain.

In Nigeria, I grew up hearing people say “sorry” when someone fell, got hurt, or had something unfortunate happen to them. It was not necessarily an admission that they had caused the problem. It was often a simple way of expressing sympathy, so I learned to use it without thinking much about it. I remember being in Canada when I saw a stranger running on a staircase. He tripped and hurt himself, and I instinctively said, “Sorry.” He looked at me and asked why I was apologizing when I had not done anything to him. I felt embarrassed and confused because his response was the last thing I expected. To me, my words had already communicated concern. To him, they seemed to suggest that I had done something wrong. There was a long, uncomfortable silence. I did not want to make the situation more awkward by explaining the cultural meaning of one word, so I said that I hoped he would get well soon and left.

02 / THE SILENCE

The longest part of the conversation was what I didn't say.

The encounter made me notice how much meaning depends on the audience rather than the dictionary definition of a word. I was speaking English to someone who also spoke English, but we were interpreting the situation through different everyday habits. My purpose was to show empathy, and my stance was concerned rather than apologetic. Yet because the conversation was spontaneous and face-to-face, I had no prepared explanation or time to rethink my choice before the stranger reacted. His question changed the tone of what I thought would be a small moment of kindness. The genre mattered too: this was not an apology letter or a formal discussion; it was a brief encounter between strangers in which the meaning of a short response had to be understood immediately. I also think the fact that I did not know him affected what I said next. If it had been someone close to me, I might have explained my intention more comfortably. With a stranger, I chose to leave the misunderstanding unresolved.

03 / WHAT CHANGED

The words changed.
The concern didn't.

After that experience, I started using different expressions when someone hurt themselves. I am now more likely to ask, “Are you okay?” or say, “I hope you weren’t hurt,” rather than automatically saying “sorry.” I did not change because I stopped believing that people deserve sympathy. I changed because I learned that people may not hear my concern in the words I grew up using. That is a small example of how moving between cultures has made me more aware of my audience, even in situations where I speak without planning. It also makes me wonder how many ordinary expressions carry meanings that people around us do not recognize. I still associate “sorry” with concern because of my upbringing, but I now have another way to express that same value. The choice is rhetorical: I adjust the wording to preserve my purpose.

04 / ON THE PHONE

Just a joke—until someone else heard it.

Another experience in Canada showed me a very different response to a misunderstanding. I was on a phone call with my brother, and we were joking and challenging each other the way we often do. At one point I said, “Abeg, shift! You fit shout? I will show you pepper today! Oya, bring your card out make we see!” The words were playful to us, even though they might sound confrontational to someone unfamiliar with the expressions. My Canadian roommate was nearby and heard me talking. He asked why I wanted to put pepper on my brother. I laughed because I had not considered that he might take “show you pepper” literally. I explained that I did not mean actual pepper or physical harm. In that exchange, I meant that I was jokingly going to give my brother a hard time or win our challenge. My roommate found the explanation funny, and he told me he liked my accent and the way I spoke. Instead of ending in discomfort, that misunderstanding became a conversation about a part of my culture.

05 / TWO AUDIENCES

The listener changes the meaning.

What makes that phone call interesting is that my brother was the person I intended to communicate with, but my roommate became an unintended audience. With my brother, the genre was an informal, teasing conversation built on our shared relationship. My tone was loud and playful, and my purpose was to joke rather than threaten him. Because he understood the expressions and our relationship, I did not need to explain every phrase. The phone call as a medium also mattered: my roommate could hear me speaking, but he did not have the same access to my brother’s replies or the history behind our jokes. Without that context, “I will show you pepper” sounded literal to him. I had to shift from teasing my brother to explaining my language to my roommate. The words themselves did not change, but my stance did—from playful competitor to patient interpreter. His curiosity gave me room to explain what the expression meant instead of treating his confusion as a problem.

06 / CHOOSING TO EXPLAIN

Not every misunderstanding needs a new vocabulary.

Comparing these experiences has made me think about when I choose to adapt and when I choose to explain. At the staircase, I stayed quiet because I felt embarrassed and did not know the stranger well enough to continue the conversation. Afterward, I changed the words I used in similar situations. During the phone call, I felt comfortable laughing and explaining because my roommate was familiar to me and seemed curious rather than upset. That conversation reminded me that the way my brother and I speak is part of our relationship, not just a collection of slang phrases. The joking tone, rhythm, and expressions help us recognize each other’s intentions. I also appreciated that my roommate noticed my accent positively. Living away from Nigeria can sometimes make familiar habits stand out in ways I did not expect, but standing out does not always mean I have to hide them. Sometimes my choice is to make my language clearer, and sometimes my choice is to invite another person to understand it.

EPILOGUE / REFLECTION

Sometimes I adapt.
Sometimes I explain.

Neither choice makes my voice less mine.

Before moving abroad, I rarely had to think about why these familiar expressions made sense to me. The staircase encounter taught me that even an attempt to show concern can be misunderstood, while the phone call with my brother showed me that an unfamiliar expression can also start a friendly conversation. Both moments were shaped by audience, relationship, purpose, tone, and the fact that they happened in spoken conversation rather than on a page. I have learned that I do not always need to respond to cultural differences in the same way: sometimes I change my words, and sometimes I explain them. What matters to me is that I can keep the care, humor, and connection behind my Nigerian expressions even as I learn how to communicate in new places.

For my language autoethnography, I wanted my classmates to understand that speaking the same language does not always mean we attach the same meaning to words. I tell two stories from my time in Canada: when a stranger questioned why I said “sorry” after he fell, and when my roommate misunderstood me joking with my brother over the phone. One language choice I made was keeping the expression “I will show you pepper” instead of immediately replacing it with “I will beat you.” I wanted classmates to hear how the phrase actually sounds in my everyday speech, and then understand the context. One detail I chose to include was the long silence after the stranger questioned my use of “sorry.” I think that silence matters because it shows how embarrassed and uncertain I felt, even though my intention was kind. A risk I took was sharing expressions that might sound aggressive or unusual without their Nigerian context. I tried to address that by including a reconstructed conversation and side-by-side explanations. I have not included a specific classmate response yet, so I plan to ask whether the phrase initially sounds threatening and whether my explanation changes that impression. I will revise this after peer review. Overall, I wanted the essay to show that sometimes I adapt my words so others understand me, and sometimes I explain my words so others understand where I come from.
